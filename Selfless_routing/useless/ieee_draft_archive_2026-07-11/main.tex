\title{Selfless Traffic Routing with Multi-Agent PPO\\in SUMO: When Does Sacrificing Help?}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anh Vu}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Department of Computer Science\\
University of Houston}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Most navigation systems route each vehicle independently along its own shortest
path. When many vehicles make that same locally optimal choice, the shared roads
they converge on become bottlenecks and total travel time rises---the gap between
the selfish (Wardrop) equilibrium and the system optimum that traffic theory calls
the price of anarchy. \emph{Selfless routing} asks a subset of vehicles to accept a
longer personal route when doing so lowers travel time for the whole fleet. We study
whether a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement-learning policy can learn this
behavior in a realistic simulator. We implement Multi-Agent Proximal Policy
Optimization (MAPPO) with centralized training and decentralized execution: a shared
per-candidate route actor chooses among up to four complete routes, and a centralized
critic that sees a fleet-wide summary stabilizes learning. Cooperation is induced by
a difference-reward team term measured directly in seconds of travel time, so the
policy can weigh personal delay against fleet delay without an arbitrary conversion.
We train the policy on a congested New York City corridor (450 controlled and 150
background vehicles routed to a shared destination) and evaluate it against a Dijkstra
shortest-path baseline. On 20 held-out scenarios, the deployed greedy policy completes
100\% of vehicles and reduces mean fleet travel time from 596.6\,s to 559.2\,s
($-6.3\%$, winning 16/20 scenarios), with lower 90th-percentile travel time and fewer
deadline misses; the improvement grows with congestion ($r=-0.48$). An ablation of a
congestion-aware detour guard reveals that the learned policy in fact prefers a
non-shortest route 48\% of the time: executing those detours lowers mean travel time
further ($-11.2\%$) by rescuing the most congested scenarios, yet hurts light-traffic
scenarios where shortest paths are already near-optimal. The result is a concrete
demonstration that the value of selfless routing tracks the network's price of anarchy,
and that a simple coordination guardrail trades peak fleet gains for robustness.
\end{abstract}
