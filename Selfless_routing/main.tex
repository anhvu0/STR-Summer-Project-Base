\begin{abstract}
Most navigation systems route each vehicle along its own shortest path; when many
vehicles make the same locally optimal choice, the roads they converge on become
bottlenecks and total travel time rises, opening the gap between the selfish (Wardrop)
equilibrium and the system optimum known as the price of anarchy. \emph{Selfless
routing} asks some vehicles to accept a longer personal route when doing so lowers
travel time for the whole fleet. We study whether cooperative multi-agent
reinforcement learning can learn this behavior in a realistic simulator: Multi-Agent
Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO) with centralized training and decentralized
execution, a shared actor that scores up to four complete candidate routes, and a
difference-reward team term measured in seconds of travel time, with a congestion-gated
incentive that pays for a detour only when it relieves a real jam. Trained on a congested
New York City corridor (450 controlled and 150 background vehicles) and evaluated against a
Dijkstra shortest-path baseline on 20 disjoint held-out scenarios, the deployed greedy
policy completes 100\% of vehicles and reduces mean fleet travel time from 596.6\,s to
534.3\,s ($-10.4\%$, winning 15/20 scenarios, paired Wilcoxon $p{=}0.02$), with lower
90th-percentile travel time and fewer deadline misses; it executes genuine load-spreading
detours (38\% non-shortest choices), and the improvement grows with congestion
($r=-0.57$), reaching $-27\%$ on a more severely congested block.
Ablating the congestion-aware detour guard, which reverts only pile-on detours, gains a
further $\sim$3\% concentrated entirely at high congestion, and a fixed-demand penetration
sweep shows the typical gain growing monotonically with the controlled share of the fleet.
The value of selfless routing tracks the network's price of anarchy, so it is largest
exactly where congestion is worst.
\end{abstract}
